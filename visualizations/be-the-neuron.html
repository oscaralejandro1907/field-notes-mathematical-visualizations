Level 1 · You are the neuron

Approve or reject? Set the weights.

A bank has 12 past applicants and knows who repaid. Each applicant has two inputs: a payment-history score and a debt level, both from 0 to 10. Your neuron multiplies each input by a weight, adds them up with the bias, and approves the loan when the total z is 0 or more.

GOAL

Classify all 12 applicants correctly. The neuron starts at 6 of 12.

THINK FIRST
  1. Payment history: should a better record push toward Approve or Reject? That decides the sign of w₁.
  2. Debt level: should more debt push toward Approve or Reject? That decides the sign of w₂.
  3. Bias: start at b = 0. Too many approvals? Move b down (stricter). Too many rejections? Move b up.

Level 2 · Rosenblatt's learning rule (1958)

Let the neuron fix its own weights.

Nobody sets the weights now. The neuron starts with bad weights and loops through the applicants: it makes a prediction, compares it with the real outcome, and nudges each weight only when it is wrong. One full pass over the 12 applicants is one epoch.

RULE
new weight = old weight + 0.05 × error × input
error
= real − predicted (1 = repaid or approve, 0 = defaulted or reject)
  • +1 if it rejected someone who repaid
  • −1 if it approved someone who defaulted
  • 0 if it was right: nothing changes
input
= that applicant's value: payment history for w₁, debt level for w₂, always 1 for the bias.
0.05
= the learning rate, the size of each correction.

Level 3 · Where one neuron breaks

A coffee farm. Can one straight line do it?

A cooperative records 17 past harvests with two inputs: average temperature and rainfall, scaled 0 to 10. The harvest is good only when both are moderate, so the good harvests sit in the middle.

CHALLENGE

Get the best score you can. Then explain why 17 of 17 is impossible here.

HOW TO PLAY

  2. Press Let it learn for 50 epochs and compare its score with yours.

Level 4 · How a network sees an image

To a computer, a picture is a grid of numbers.

Each square of the grid is one pixel: 1 if it has ink, 0 if it is empty. The first layer of an image network slides small filters over the picture. A filter is a neuron with 9 weights that lights up when it finds one pattern, such as a vertical edge.

CHALLENGE

Draw a shape that lights up the vertical-edge map but leaves the horizontal-edge map almost dark.

HOW TO PLAY
  1. Click or drag on the grid to draw. Click an inked square to erase it.
  2. Watch the two maps: darker blue means a stronger edge found there.
  3. Tap a square in a map to see the 9 multiplications behind it.
